How Unity Sells and Polarized Diversity Fractures Through Social Media


Unity, when packaged for public consumption, quickly transforms into a marketable image, one that appeals to the global gaze.

In large political movements, especially when they attract worldwide attention, representation becomes crucial and is expressed in three forms: the image of the demonstrators, the image of what they fight for, and the image of what they fight against. In the Iranian demonstrations of early 2026,1 only one of these images seems to be decided without doubt, though it is arguably the most important one. The complexity of representing the other two at such a critical historical moment naturally exposes diversity.

Unity on social platforms, however, alongside polarization, becomes an algorithmic product. These platforms are structurally designed to canalize this natural diversity into two or a few poles, ones that are easy to recognize, label, and share within homogenized circles, ones that are easy to identify and attack, often violently. This algorithmic canalization of diversity helps social platforms win engagement and enables groups operating within identity politics to quickly spread information or disinformation. In this movement, however, this canalization fractures the one image that was already clear.

The image of the demonstrators

For the world to listen to the demonstrators and act, there must first be reliability, then relatability. Reliability can be physical as well as analytical. Outsiders physically trust demonstrators who act peacefully and are not threatening. They analytically trust those who are committed to fact-checking and who avoid obvious fallacies and exaggerations.

Relatability, on the other hand, is both easier and more complex to think of. Easy when it’s about systematic omission of what’s less popular. At times it’s a systematic whitewash, like when your public does not feel comfortable to relate to racism, the colorfulness of the LGBTQ+ flags conceals the dirty image to buy a far larger audience. Other times it functions as a smart amplification. When the Iranian community mourns more loudly for a blonde motorcycle-riding girl than for an elderly man wearing the clothing of an Iranian minority, the Global North relates better.2

More complex is when that same relatable image is clouded by flags that the very audience starting to trust does not support. The image grows more distant because it requires digging deeper into background and context. Depending on the context, a symbol must be culturally learned. A flag operates as a symbol.

When the flags of countries guilty of war crimes are waved in opposition to activism aimed at stopping those crimes, the same flag does not carry the same meaning as when it is waved to call for immediate help to stop human rights violations. Although this distinction does not allow the symbol to circumvent criticism, the question remains whether a skeptical outsider audience can escape the trap of contextomy. Contextomy is an image, cropped perfectly to prove a point.

The image of what is fought for (the catalyzer)

Thinking about what you fight for is thinking about the futures you choose. That decision is not easy when one is mentally and physically threatened. However, appearing decisive represents a more homogenized community to an outsider audience that may already have doubts. How Iranian demonstrators acted upon it reminds me of the two categories of normative ethics: consequentialists vs. deontologists.

The first group, consequentialists, focus on pleasant outcomes. They support a clear, easily digestible image as their representative, an image whose recognition could catalyze an end to immediate human suffering.

The second group, deontologists, believe that principles and values generally agreed upon as good and true, such as democracy, must not be compromised at any cost. They see the danger in short-term gains that demand long-term sacrifices.

One group takes a pragmatic view of the person framed as representative; the other scrutinizes that person’s background and rejects any misalignment with their values. In many cases, this leads to rejecting the person entirely, not just the misalignment.

But there is a third group within the demonstrators, though outside this dichotomy: fascists. In contrast to those who reject a person completely, they subordinate all values unconditionally to a single individual. Ironically, both extremes abandon skepticism, denying alternative possibilities the chance to live. However, before one gets too excited about this categorization that imprisons diverse opinions to make them digestible, before one identifies with one camp and attacks the other, I break the categories myself and invite cross-pollination.

How the two images shape Collective Memories

Media functions as technologies of collective memory, drawing from Bernard Stiegler’s essays on technologies of memory.3 It tells united communities what to collectively remember as a common past and how to build a dream of the future on top of that. Media double as the tools of collecting memories and their environments, reinforcing ways of remembering, while also speeding up transindividuation.

Transindividuation, primarily developed by the philosopher Gilbert Simondon,4 refers to the mutually constitutive process in which individual psychic development and collective social development occur simultaneously and in relation to each other. The recent transindividuation of the “united we” in Iranian communities must be studied in relation to the Pahlavi–Netanyahu–Trump assemblage. The practice of studying transindividuation with assemblages instead of individual authorities helps us better understand the collection of symbols that form one identity.

In this assemblage, Trump has a mostly pragmatic presence, while Netanyahu has a mostly identificational presence. This explains how this identification benefits both the Iranian and Israeli sides of this assemblage, while American Republican citizens are mostly absent. The assemblage expropriates and reuses symbols that fit and feed the identity, as building symbols from scratch is costly and time-consuming; within these expropriations, symbols transform in meaning.

The Pahlavi–Netanyahu identification finds expression in the rejection of terrorist groups and Islamic extremism, in “civil” behavior, and in LGBTQ+ flags, which in some cases function negationally. This becomes visible in the amplification and framing of civil behavior regarding the police, not in a behavioral-adoptive sense but in a racialized one, as a response to recently circulated incidents of conflict during pro-Palestinian demonstrations. These constitute a form of identification through racial negation. In many videos showing how Iranians behaved during demonstrations in the host country, the word “Persian” (which excludes non-Persian Iranians) is used instead of “Iranian.”

Another instance of this negational identification among Iranians is the “Woman, Life, Freedom” slogan, which began with the 2022 “Woman, Life, Freedom” movement inside and outside Iran.5 Although it has been a symbol of resistance, it has turned into a symbol of the political left among Iranians. This happened through a negational identification of the new “united we,” produced by this assemblage, and has largely decided to no longer associate or resonate with this slogan.

Seen in this light, the circulation of these techno-mediated memories among Israeli–Iranian media emphasizes the importance of identity politics in uniting individuals on social media (the image of the demonstrators) over mutual goals (the image of what is fought for).

Unity vs. unification vs. multiplicities

Unity is the state of being in harmony as one, while unification is the process of bringing separate parts together into one. The French philosophers Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari, however, reject both as deterministic descriptions of any process.6 They argue that identification and unity come first, followed by differentiation.

Politically interpreted, people first identify with a community, what it represents, what it fights for, what it fights against, and the policies it supports. Once this identification consolidates, differentiation begins at both scales. The collective includes and excludes; people connect and disconnect.

Meanwhile, the desire to connect and unite is functioning as a constructing machine, multiplicities still keep their individuality and do not convert into parts of a whole. Once the desire and recognition have been strong enough to form a fresh identity, it’s time that the formation fractures, groups and individuals denounce that bigger identity and flow forward.

What social platforms block is the natural movement from unity to differentiation. They polarize and overfeed the poles to such an extent that mobility of thought and receptivity become nearly impossible. They help construct this fresh unity, but when it comes to the second movement, instead of exposing differences, they canalize diversity into poles.

This threat is algorithmically embedded in the apparatus of social platforms. But the user is not programmed to surrender to it. The user still has the power to break this internal blockade, to change platforms, or at least to engage and disengage deliberately within their structures.
