Speaking Through the Citations:
Wikipedia and Epistemic Traceability

Abstract

How does anonymity under the “NPOV” (neutral point of view) policy redistribute traceability and epistemic responsibility in Wikipedia’s knowledge production?

This essay proposes a way of visualizing Wikipedia’s collaborative production of knowledge as a shifting field of relations, drawing on Strathern’s idea of opening up relations (2018) and Haraway’s concept of situated knowledge (1988).

The Inquiry

There is a virtual universe, a platform, that once promised us the emancipation of cognitive capitalism, or platform capitalism. Wikipedia appears among the first suggestions in most Google searches, across almost any imaginable topic, if enough contributors have recognized the topic. It is also ranked second among the top websites used to train large language models (Schaul et al., 2023). A platform we imagine we can choose to step outside of, but is sneakily inescapable, so inescapable that even teachers have stopped warning students of “no Wikipedia as a source.”

In theory, its welcoming attitude towards diverse contributions is part of its success story: the heartwarming message that it does not reject you even as a newbie, no matter your educational background, status, class, affiliation, or income. In practice, however, the dream of a non-exclusive Wikipedia is one that challenges Wikipedia’s autopoiesis. It is a paradoxical dream in which its realization depends on the undoing of the very structure that sustains it. Your anonymous existence on Wikipedia as a user must be somehow rated with a virtue system that ensures you are not a threat to its ecosystem. And although time can be a luxury that the minority of Wikipedia editors can afford, it remains a scarce resource. The idea of saving time, delegating editing labor to bots and restricting vandalizers that history shows had no purpose but to start edit wars, is a way of valuing the community’s scarce time.

If this utopian idea of any anonymous user being able to meaningfully participate is a threat to the system in practice, let us deliberately attempt to rewrite that idea. To rewrite this idea is to work at the same speed as Wikipedia’s own edits. The possibility of any utopia of openness must be tested against the temporal logic of the platform itself, its capacity to revise, revert, and re-stabilize meaning through continuous collaborative action. This aligns with the community of practice as Wenger and Lave describe (Lave & Wenger, 1991). The reification of knowledge production through documentation, policies, editorial norms, and the creation of an encyclopedia is a snapshot that, while you’re analyzing it has already moved on to the next frame.

The resulting stratification does not eliminate anonymity; it differentiates it. Anonymous participation becomes internally graded through traces left in edit histories, producing a system in which anonymity is no longer a condition of absence; it is a spectrum of visibility.

Anonymity is a widely accepted Wikipedia guideline, as it does not require you to provide personal information on user pages or elsewhere. This is intended to create a safer environment for contributors, preventing them from getting into trouble simply by advocating their side of the truth. It should, however, not be seen as a binary, as it is a choice left to the user how much of their identity lives on their Wikipedia page, or they can as well get outed by other users. Because a user's interest areas, writing style, and argumentative positions may establish an identifiable pattern, true pseudonymity may not be realistically achievable in our episteme (Wikipedia contributors, n.d., Wikipedia: Personal security practices).

But when the anonymous user is responsible for a claim, the credibility and trackability of that claim becomes a crucial question, especially if one is to remain committed to the NPOV policy. Assuming a single universal truth may be a naïve interpretation of that commitment. Instead, inclusivity (speaking of all sides) and traceability (being able to situate claims) might be a more convincing way to understand Wikipedia’s epistemic norm.

Wikipedia’s founder Jimmy Wales has been repeatedly emphasizing the inclusivity of points of view from all sides in response to critics assuming it more left-leaning than neutral. But in practice, how could an article ever end up exactly in the middle of a spectrum? It can’t.

Neutral point of view is, of course, one of Wikipedia’s core principles. Yet history has long shown that no claim is ever fully objective or neutral. What operates here as “neutrality” is something else: not an absence of position, but an immanence that emerges through collective practices and the platform’s technology.

To “assert facts” in this context does not mean asserting one’s own view as truth, but to assert what can be attributed and cited. Facts become facts about positions, about who says what, rather than direct expressions of an individual opinion. This is reinforced by another core rule: the prohibition of original research.

Just like in a game, the practice of neutrality is the sensitivity to the discriminatory possibility of the technical and the social assemblages, although one side might end up winning (Reagle, 2010).

Donna Haraway challenges the obsession with objectivity and shifts the focus toward responsibility (Haraway, 1988). No matter who you are, your claims are always situated; rather than denying this, we should make it as explicit as possible. We should be able to trace a claim back to who made it, from which position, with what background, and through which frameworks of language.

At first glance, this tracing appears impossible, since Wikipedia editors are anonymous. Has the conception of responsibility and traceability of knowledge claims already been aborted before our mission even begins?

Yet this is not the full story.

Claims, when properly added, are tied to references. When they are not, but appear factual, they are often flagged on “Talk” or “Discussion” pages, where others call for sources. What follows is a collective effort to locate an origin, or to question whether the claim can stand at all. Responsibility, then, may fall onto a dispersed group of people who do not even know they are being cited. Still, the citation tells us something: that the cited source mirrors the “point of view” of the anonymous editor, who is, in principle, not allowed one!

Yet this is not quite the form of accountability that Haraway points toward, since the one making the claim is not the one intentionally holding themselves accountable. This raises the question of whether accountability is always, or even immediately, possible only after such intentionality.

In 2003, a Wikipedian proposed an informal policy, “Accountability,” understood as “having an account, to be responded to on your user talk page,” as a way to counter the effects of anonymity. Some years later, this shifted toward a different formulation: that editors should explain the reasoning behind their edits, especially when making questionable changes.

The proposal, however, never stabilized as policy. Perhaps the multiplicity of reasons that could be given was reduced under the binary of “good faith” and “bad faith.”

When we trace the claim backward, we walk past the editor and arrive at the references, which can be a global or local news medium, book, organization, or individual. This tracing can produce the impression of a hierarchy that should be questioned. Because the connections exist even before we sift the dust to reconstruct them, and the relation between subject and object of knowledge precedes the constitution of knowledge itself.

Marilyne Strathern paints a different dynamic of the subject-object divide in knowledge making, drawn from Luhmann’s systems theory. One that is different from the idea of bits of knowledge being domains of a whole truth. In this dynamic, subject and object are produced within specific relational configurations, and knowledge doubles as the subject being a means of knowledge making, and its object being what we study or want to know more of (Strathern, 2018).

Knowledge production emerges through relations that simultaneously constitute both what counts as subject and what counts as object of study. Through this emergence, entities that were once objects of study reappear as actants, now functioning as tools and ways of thinking through other objects of study.

As Donna Haraway famously writes:
"It matters what thoughts think thoughts. It matters what knowledges know knowledges” (Haraway, 2016).

Those ways of thinking, the means of making, have themselves once been treated as ends. But, ironically, what we call an end is never final, as there is no final objective claim that everyone can agree on.

The flow doesn’t end with the objects of knowledge, the made claims, as it reverses and flows back to the preliminary and even other subjects and means to redefine and refine or complicate the product of knowledge making, over and over again. In Scardamalia and Bereiter’s framework, shared ideas become “objects of inquiry” that inhabit a life of their own within discourse-oriented processes, once shared on the platform (Scardamalia & Bereiter, 2003).

How Strathern conceptualizes knowledge as relational expansion requires us to socio-materially analyze collaborative knowledge-making practices. Sociomateriality theory emphasizes the centrality of materials within the communicative constitution of organizations (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). In Wikipedia, this type of analysis sketches the interactions between three layers: first, the editor (the subject layer); second, the article (the object layer); and finally, a layer that remains fluid between subject and object; the means of study, the resources and citations, those “knowledges that know knowledges”. This is the layer through which the editor’s contribution is always mediated.

Haraway describes this mediation as follows:
“Vision requires instruments of vision; an optics is a politics of positioning. Instruments of vision mediate standpoints; there is no immediate vision from the standpoints of the subjugated” (Haraway, 1988).

It’s the agency of this in-between layer that sets the potentiality for the emergence of editor collaboration, including both consensus and conflict (Keegan et al., 2011).

The process on Wikipedia starts with an editor, situated in their own positionality and perspective, which already relates them to the article. They appeal to authorities and external sources as a way of referring to the object of knowledge and shaping the final product of each iteration.

Wikipedia structurally encourages such outsourcing due to its policies against original research. As a result, the object of inquiry is not the editor’s interpretation itself, but the text that emerges through citation, with only a limited scope for subjective framing.

The process continues not by moving forward, but backwards to another or the same editor, disagreeing with parts or the whole claim or with the sufficiency of it.

Figure 1. Editing interactions between two Wikipedia users.

This loop either slightly or fully shifts, with its visible product (the text) shifting as well when written from scratch. For Strathern, this entire process constitutes knowledge, not just what it visibly produces. All vertices of this circulation, which determine the angle and direction of these flows, all the flows of relations between them, must be accounted as agents (Haraway, 1988).

One can imagine the object as an agent that reflects back on the subject, while the subject itself shifts positionality over time. In this movement, what is reflected returns to the subject in the future, encountered from a new position that enables this very receptivity. These loops in the practice of making knowledge therefore split the subject.

Figure 2. Editing loops as recursive subject–object relations.

The emergence of loops highlights the temporal properties of one article, as well as Wikipedia’s broader knowledge structure, meaning those loops that emerge between articles. These loops are produced through hyperlinks, which connect articles in ways that are systematic rather than random. Rather than forming a simple tree structure, Wikipedia approximates a hub-based network. In such a structure, a small number of central nodes are connected to many others, while new nodes tend to attach themselves again to these central points. As a result, knowledge is organized around hubs rather than branching hierarchies (Muchnik et al., 2007).

For example, the article on physics links to electricity, which in turn links to hydrodynamics, but this is not the only path between them. Multiple short paths exist through different intermediary concepts. This redundancy means that changes in one domain, such as a revision of hydrodynamics, can propagate through the network and affect a wide range of related concepts within physics.

The first type of loops is temporal and emerges through iterations of claim-making. The second type is spatial and visible when we zoom out to observe the macro-scale knowledge structure of Wikipedia. The focus here is on loops that emerge temporally, and that’s why we need to focus on a smaller scale, as in SNA language, the “egonet” scale, a decentralized egonet. Temporal loops appear through editors’ collaboration practices. These collaborations are often conflictual rather than affirmative.

When editors disagree on the interpretation of an event, observing it through two opposing political standpoints, does the article ever reach epistemic equilibrium?

Conflict has been a core part of Wikipedia, and it has been acknowledged in social network analysis of the platform through methods like signed interaction networks (Maniu et al., 2013).

In a virtual community where “POV pushing” is common, meaning the accusation that someone is adding their own point of view, pushing for NPOV itself is a source of conflict (Reagle, 2010). The editor that loses the edit war might have wished a thoroughly rewritten article based on their reality. Like a virtual parallel article existing only for them, and if someone asks how they relate to the topic, they can just share the link to that parallel repository.

So, are we still on the same page?

A 2025 study has found that biographical articles about women arrive significantly at less consensus across different languages than articles about men, and this gap grows when we look at those articles for people from the Global South (Yu et al., 2025). In a literal sense, different cultures are not “on the same page” when it comes to objects of knowledge, for example, representations of women figures from the Global South. In other words, certain objects of inquiry are culturally approached and represented in ways that are not fully translatable across contexts; they are multiplied.

Figure 3. We are not on the same page.

Marilyn Strathern sees debate as non-productive when approached from two different worlds and sees conflicts as conflicts of worlds not just conflicts of perspectives that can find their peaceful common grounds with negotiation.

The non-productive debates are fruitful in the way that they are proliferative. The fruition of editor collaboration is not consensus; it is more emerging conflicts. The proliferative debates leave their traces in time, within a space not called “common ground”, but where patterns of inscription and erasure arise. Those patterns show us how responsibility is distributed across this space.

Data collection methods

To observe these patterns, I analyze four highly contested Wikipedia articles that are prone to vandalism. These are controversial articles on sensitive topics that are semi-protected, in order to examine possible hierarchies among editors. The chosen articles were the “Gaza War”, the “Iraq War”, the “Russo-Ukrainian War”, and the “September 11 attacks”, among the most frequently edited articles related to recent conflicts. In such extreme cases, high-intensity editing increases the visibility of collaborative and conflictual patterns.

Knowing that the “Gaza War” is the most recent conflict on the list, it is also the most frequently edited one, averaging approximately 5.43 edits per day. This is followed by the September 11 attacks at around 1.55 edits per day, the Russo-Ukrainian war at 0.95, and the Iraq War at 0.87.

I decided to immerse myself in the messy edit history. Other approaches—such as web scraping or semantic filtering—might have been faster, but also more error-prone and more distanced from the texture of edits themselves. Wikipedia’s revision history offers only partial traces of what happens to an article, often through short editor summaries that describe what was changed. Yet these summaries are uneven: sometimes they are absent altogether, or a “visual edit” compresses multiple small interventions into a single gesture.

So, I chose a slower method, one that stays close to the material unfolding of edits themselves. I went through the history manually, edit by edit:

• following the revision history over a given span (defined not by time, but by frequency of editing)
• filtering changes above 100 bytes
• distinguishing whether references are added with or without new claims attached

Sometimes editors add a claim without any citation but call for others’ cooperation by writing “citation needed.” Sometimes the opposite occurs, where there is a resource update without a claim. Both cases were not included in this exploration.

A socio-cognitive and socio-material analysis practice requires attending to how editors attempt to tweak the object of knowledge through practices of collaborative production, while remaining attentive to their positionalities. This is what turns our 2D space into a critical field of maneuver.

For this, I mapped the objects onto a 2D bias–factuality spectrum, where the X-axis represents political bias (left on the left, right on the right) and the Y-axis accounts for factuality (most factual at the bottom, least at the top). From this point on, I use “objects” to refer to the means of knowledge-making, here, the external references (news sources, organizations). They are treated as such because they have previously functioned as objects of study, each produced through their own modes of knowing.

This usage should not be confused with the subject–object distinction outlined earlier in the theoretical framework.

Hyperlinks are not treated here as editorial means of approaching an article, since they remain internal to the platform and circulate within the same set of practices. Instead, this analysis prioritizes external references, as it aims to trace objects of knowledge back to those who can be held responsible for their production, in a Harawayian sense.

The Bias and Factuality Rating

In this research, I used the website Media Bias/Fact Check (MBFC), a U.S.-based website that rates news sources’ bias and credibility. This choice was made because MBFC offers one of the largest sets of labels among news source rating services (Bozarth et al., 2020), and research has shown that using MBFC instead of other lists usually does not significantly change results (Bozarth, Saraf, and Budak 2020). MBFC is widely used in computational social science as a source of bias and factuality annotations for news outlets, and it has already been used successfully to analyze Wikipedia citations (Yang & Colavizza, 2023).

The measures incorporated in this research include bias ratings, factuality of reports, country, medium name, and categorization. Among these, bias is operationalized through MBFC, which locates each medium on a spectrum ranging from extreme left to extreme right, assigning scores on a scale from -10 to +10.

Their method is based on reviewing a minimum of five full news stories from each source and evaluating bias across four dimensions: economic positioning (35%), social liberalism versus conservatism (35%), balance in straight news reporting (15%), and editorial bias (15%). What follows from this is that bias is assessed at the level of the outlet as a whole, rather than at the level of individual articles, meaning that the positioning of a source does not necessarily align with the specific report cited.

For factual reporting, MBFC evaluates the extent to which a source provides accurate and factually reliable information, rating media as very high, high, mostly factual, mixed, low, or very low. This assessment is based on a weighted combination of failed fact checks (40%), sourcing (25%), transparency (25%), and one-sidedness or omission (10%), each scored on a scale from 0 to 10.

Within this research, sources are further categorized as global or local media, organizations or foundations, academic journals or books, and official sources. While most news media and organizations fall within MBFC’s scope, its coverage remains partial, leaving a range of sources outside its evaluative framework.

Academic sources are treated here as less overtly partisan, though this does not imply absence of bias. These sections were left unassigned, under the assumption that they would not significantly skew the overall credibility rating of an article. This assumption, however, does not extend to government sources, which may operate within ideological or propagandistic frameworks. Their absence therefore marks a limitation of this method.

MBFC’s left–right political spectrum is U.S.-centric and does not map neatly onto European political configurations. Given that English Wikipedia users are not confined to a U.S. context, these ratings do not necessarily resonate across all epistemic settings.

Just as this research critically examines the means through which knowledge is produced on the platform, MBFC itself must be treated as part of that same field of scrutiny. It is not a neutral instrument. Its American point of view must be taken into account, as well as the fact that outlet-level ratings may differ from the specific articles cited in Wikipedia.

Visualization

How is it even possible to visualize a social graph if each editor is building their own world from within a situated standpoint? Would this require multiple layers and does it resist comparison altogether?

Just as Wikipedia itself might have remained a dream had it stayed loyal to its initial rules, consider the principle of “ignore all rules”; research cannot afford a similar paralysis. These micro-attempts to decode and recodify social practices, while remaining attentive to situatedness, are what sustain movement toward emancipation from cognitive capitalism.

In this sense, the following diagrams should not be read as objective maps, but as analytical tools.

In these diagrams, triangles represent sources (news outlets, organizations, etc.):
• Their horizontal position (X-axis) reflects political bias
• Their vertical position (Y-axis) reflects factual reliability (bottom = more factual, top = less factual)

Circles represent Wikipedia editors, and their positions depend on their connections.

Green lines indicate when an editor adds a source to an article. Pink lines indicate when an editor removes a source.

The (0,0) circle represents the most factual, least biased sources, expanded for better readability.

Figure 4. Socio-material network graphs.

Across all four articles, the shape is similar: an arrow pointing toward the top right corner, dense in the bottom left and thinning out as it moves upward and to the right.

The “Gaza War” article shifts slightly from this pattern. It becomes more rectangular, heavier on the left, with more density in the top left than the top right. Most interactions, additions and removals, cluster around left-leaning sources, even those with lower factuality compared to the other graphs.

In all four articles, there are many editors, particularly those with higher out-degree, whose interactions stretch in different directions. You might expect them to consistently align with one political side and remove opposing sources, but that is not always the case.

This complicates the assumption that editors operate as stable ideological actors.

There are counterexamples. In the “September 11 attacks” article, two editors form a diagonal axis: one removes less factual sources and adds more factual ones. The most central editor removes four citations spread across different positions and adds three that are relatively more neutral and more factual.

Looking at connectivity around sources, some appear only in removals or only in additions. Others appear in both. The first group shows no conflict around their inclusion or exclusion. Interestingly, some of these non-conflict sources sit at the extremes of low factuality.

For example, in the “Gaza War” article, at the very top of the spectrum, where factuality is lowest, three citations were added without triggering any conflict.

Figure 5. Socio-material network graph of the Gaza War article.
Figure 6. Comparison of source-removal and source-adding graphs.

In the distinct source-removal and source-adding graphs, there are central editors with higher out-degrees than others, more visibly present in the “Gaza War” article, possibly due to the overall density of the graph.

In the “Russo-Ukrainian War” article, most activity converges on two central nodes. When comparing their source-adding and source-removal graphs, both shift toward the bottom left, reflecting the political position of the references they tend to agree with.

The following analytical findings focus on a snapshot of the object layer (resources) for each article, collected on 30 January 2026.

The overall correlation between political bias (X) and factual reporting (Y) among sources across all four articles is 0.1721, suggesting no strong overall relationship between the two variables. Yet a directional tendency emerges as we move toward the political right: outlets in this range tend to show lower factuality scores.

Figure 7. Bias–factuality distribution.

This also shows that the majority of mostly factual media carry a liberal bias, typically ranging between 0 and -4. The same pattern appears both in this research snapshot and in the temporal visualizations.

The “Gaza War” article exhibits the strongest liberal bias compared to the other three, with a weighted average bias (X) of -1.96 and a weighted average factuality (Y) of 1.81. This raises a methodological question: to what extent is this distribution shaped by editorial selection within Wikipedia itself, and to what extent does it reflect the bias already present in the media sources covering the event?

In either case, responsibility cannot be located solely at the level of the anonymous editor. It is dispersed across the network of sources that editors draw upon, suggesting that epistemic responsibility is co-produced.

The “Iraq War” article shows a weighted average bias (X) of -1.46 and a weighted average factuality (Y) of 1.82, closely followed by the “Russo-Ukrainian War” article with identical bias (X = -1.46) and slightly lower factuality (Y = 1.81). The “September 11 attacks” article has a weighted average bias (X) of -1.40 and the lowest factuality score among the four (Y = 1.64).

Discussion

The presented visualization method, its scale and inclusivity, is no “god trick” to be an invisible eye from nowhere, but rather the result of my limited experience with SNA, NetworkX, and physical RAM capacity. The proposed visualizations emerge from personal struggles to find a balance between how the two main theories describe the layers and agents of knowledge (those of Donna Haraway and Marilyn Strathern), and the readability of coded visual objects and patterns for the researcher or curious-minded audience.

In a way, this analysis is as productive in its attempt to reveal patterns as it is destructive, as it removes actual lived experiences on Wikipedia. I invite researchers to explore different balances, different visualization mediums, and, when possible, to expose patterns on a larger data scale.

To Conclude

The anonymity on Wikipedia doesn’t remove responsibility, and the visualizations do not resolve the tension between anonymity and traceability. They make it visible as something distributed across interactions, edits, and layers of mediation, rather than located in any single actor or moment.

The proposed socio-material network visualizations complicate the idea of knowledge-making processes and question non-mediated approaches to purely anthropocentric collaborative knowledge-making analysis. They allow us to wander among the iterative, ever-shifting, and ever-expanding socio-cognitive relations of curiosity, vision, community productivity, and belonging.

This does not lead to closure. It leads back into the next iteration of the process itself. Moving through these visualizations is like moving through uneven and conflictual relations of editing and positioning, showing how knowledge is repeatedly made.
